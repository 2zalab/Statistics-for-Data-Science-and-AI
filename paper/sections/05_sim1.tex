\section{Simulation study I: controlled loss-differential fields}\label{sec:sim1}

The first study isolates the inferential problem from the learning problem. Prediction errors are simulated directly, so that the true expected loss differential is known exactly and the dependence of the $d_i$ is controlled.

\subsection{Design}

\paragraph{Fields.} Observations lie on a $12\times12$ grid of sites observed at $24$ time points ($n=3456$). All fields are separable Gaussian fields with unit variance, exponential spatial correlation $\exp(-h/a)$ and AR(1) temporal dependence with coefficient $\phi$. Four dependence levels are considered: \emph{none} ($a=0$, $\phi=0$), \emph{weak} ($a=1$, $\phi=0.3$), \emph{moderate} ($a=2$, $\phi=0.6$) and \emph{strong} ($a=4$, $\phi=0.8$). The corresponding practical ranges are $3a=0,3,6,12$ grid units in space and $-3/\log\phi=0,2.5,5.9,13.4$ time steps; at the strong level the range is as large as the domain.

\paragraph{Losses.} With $\varepsilon$, $e_A$ and $e_B$ three independent fields, the response noise is $\varepsilon$, model $A$ has errors $e_A$ and model $B$ has errors $\sqrt{1+\delta}\,e_B$. Under squared loss,
\[
d_i=(\varepsilon_i-e_{A,i})^2-(\varepsilon_i-\sqrt{1+\delta}\,e_{B,i})^2+\eta_{k(i)},\qquad \E[d_i]=-\delta ,
\]
so that $\Delta_n=-\delta$ exactly. The fold effects $\eta_k=\sigma_\eta(\sqrt{\rho}\,g+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,h_k)$, with $g,h_1,\dots,h_K$ i.i.d. standard normal, $\sigma_\eta=0.05$ and $\rho=0.25$, mimic the training-induced variability of~\eqref{eq:model}, including its correlation across folds. We use $\delta=0$ (null) and $\delta\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4\}$ (alternatives).

\paragraph{Validation and procedures.} The domain is cut into $4\times4$-site tiles crossed with $6$-step time windows ($G=36$ blocks), which are allocated to $K=5$ folds with balanced sizes. We compare the paired $t$-test on fold means, the i.i.d. bootstrap, the block bootstrap and a random-intercept LMM (both with the $36$ blocks as clusters), the Diebold--Mariano test with a site-wise Newey--West variance (DM--HAC), the space--time Hering--Genton test, the kernel variant DA-K and the proposed DA-P. Ablations of DA-P remove the training term or replace the Satterthwaite reference by a normal distribution; a kernel estimator with a fixed bandwidth equal to the block size is also included. Bootstraps use $999$ resamples. There are $1000$ replicates per dependence level under the null and $300$ under each alternative, so that the Monte Carlo standard error of a rejection rate of $5\%$ is $0.7$ percentage points.

\paragraph{Pilot studies and choice of the variance estimator.} All design choices were made in pilot studies on random seeds disjoint from those of the main studies, whose scripts and outputs are included in the code repository. Our initial proposal was the kernel estimator DA-K, with bandwidths equal to the estimated practical ranges (multipliers between $0.5$ and $2$ changed rejection rates by at most $2.7$ points). The pilot revealed that its residual over-rejection under moderate and strong dependence came almost entirely from replicates in which $\hat\tau^2$ was truncated at zero: without the compensation provided by $\hat\tau^2$, the downward bias of the fold-centred kernel estimate (Proposition~\ref{prop:bias}) was no longer offset. Replacing the kernel by the fitted correlation model removed this problem in pilot versions of both simulation studies, with maximum pilot rejection rates of $4.0\%$ in Experiment~1 and $9.2\%$ in Experiment~2, against $16.3\%$ and $14.2\%$ for DA-K; DA-P was therefore adopted before the main studies were run.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{Type I error.} Table~\ref{tab:exp1} and Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1size} report rejection rates under the null. Procedures that ignore dependence fail quickly: the i.i.d. bootstrap rejects a true null in $19\%$ of replicates under weak dependence and in $80\%$ under strong dependence, and the time-only DM--HAC test, which ignores correlation between sites, behaves almost as badly ($17\%$ and $70\%$). The two cluster procedures, block bootstrap and LMM, drift upwards as the range of dependence approaches the block size ($15\%$ and $14\%$ at the moderate level, $27\%$ and $25\%$ at the strong level), because they treat neighbouring blocks as independent, and they reject $9\%$ and $7\%$ of true nulls even without dependence, as they ignore the correlation of fold effects. The paired $t$-test on blocked fold means, the best conventional procedure in line with Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}, reaches $11\%$ at the moderate and $18\%$ at the strong level, and the kernel variant DA-K $10\%$ and $13\%$.

DA-P keeps the rejection rate at or below the nominal level at every dependence level ($3.1\%$, $4.2\%$, $3.4\%$ and $2.4\%$); it is slightly conservative, most at the strong level, where the practical range is as long as the domain and the variance is inflated by the small number of independent regions. The space--time Hering--Genton test, whose exponential model is correctly specified in this design, is also valid ($4.4\%$, $5.5\%$, $4.4\%$, $2.6\%$). Removing the training term from DA-P increases its rejection rate by $1$--$2$ points, with a maximum of $5.7\%$; in this design the training variance is small ($\sigma_\eta=0.05$), and its role is examined in Section~\ref{sec:sim1rho} and in Experiment~2.

\paragraph{Boundary case $\hat\tau^2=0$.} Table~\ref{tab:boundary} reports the rejection rate of DA-P according to whether the training variance was truncated at zero. Truncation is frequent ($63$--$76\%$ of replicates), because the training effects are small compared with the test-sampling variability of fold means; the rejection rate in truncated replicates ($2.5$--$6.1\%$) remains close to the nominal level, and that in the other replicates is low. The large degrees of freedom obtained after truncation therefore do not cause over-rejection here; in DA-K, by contrast, truncated replicates accounted for nearly all false positives (see the pilot paragraph above).

\paragraph{Coverage, width and power.} Figure~\ref{fig:exp1cov} reports the coverage of the $95\%$ intervals separately for each true gap $\delta$ and dependence level. DA-P covers the true $\Delta_n$ in $95$--$99\%$ of replicates in every cell, and Hering--Genton in $94$--$98\%$, whereas the i.i.d. bootstrap and DM--HAC fall to $17$--$32\%$ under strong dependence and the cluster procedures to $67$--$78\%$. The DA-P intervals are wider than those of the cluster procedures (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1size}b), which is the price of validity. Raw rejection rates under the alternatives (Fig.~\ref{fig:exp1power}) are not comparable across procedures whose sizes differ by an order of magnitude; we therefore also report size-adjusted power, using as critical value the $5\%$ quantile of each procedure's null $p$-values (Table~\ref{tab:exp1power}). Under moderate dependence, DA-P, Hering--Genton and the block bootstrap have the same size-adjusted power ($46\%$ at $\delta=0.4$), above that of the paired $t$-test and DA-K ($29\%$); without dependence, DA-P ($56\%$ at $\delta=0.2$) is less efficient than the cluster procedures ($81\%$) and Hering--Genton ($63\%$), because its training term is estimated from $K-1=4$ degrees of freedom. Its slight conservativeness also lowers its raw power, for example $48\%$ against $63\%$ for Hering--Genton at $\delta=0.2$ without dependence.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the correlation of training effects}\label{sec:sim1rho}

Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} shows that the variance is underestimated when the true correlation $\rho$ of fold effects exceeds the assumed value $\rho_0$. To quantify this, we increased the training variance to $\sigma_\eta=0.1$ and varied the true $\rho\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75\}$, without and with moderate dependence ($500$ null replicates each), and ran DA-P with $\rho_0\in\{0,1/K,0.5\}$ (Table~\ref{tab:rho}). Under moderate dependence, DA-P stays at or below $5.6\%$ whatever $\rho$ and $\rho_0$, because the test-sampling variance dominates. Without spatio-temporal dependence, where the training variance is a large part of the total, DA-P is valid for $\rho\le0.25$ but rejects $10\%$ of true nulls at $\rho=0.5$ and $15\%$ at $\rho=0.75$ with the default $\rho_0=1/K$; assuming $\rho_0=0.5$ only reduces these rates to $8\%$ and $13\%$. All other procedures are worse in these settings ($14$--$29\%$). The common component of the fold effects, $\sqrt\rho\,g$ in our design, shifts all folds together and is therefore indistinguishable from a genuine difference in a single cross-validation run, as predicted by the result of Bengio and Grandvalet~\cite{bengio2004}. Highly correlated training effects arise when the training sets of different folds are nearly identical, i.e.\ with many folds, or when the learners are unstable in a way that depends on the whole sample; repeated cross-validation with different block allocations would provide information on $\rho$ and is left for future work.

\subsection{Robustness to misspecification}\label{sec:sim1robust}

DA-P assumes a separable, isotropic, stationary exponential correlation for the loss differential. Table~\ref{tab:robust} reports its behaviour, at the moderate dependence level, when this model is wrong: geometric anisotropy with ranges in ratio $4{:}1$; a Gaussian covariance $\exp(-(h/a)^2)$, smoother than the exponential; heteroscedastic errors whose standard deviation varies by a factor of about $4.5$ across the domain; a non-stationary field whose range increases from $1.5$ to $12$ grid units from one side of the domain to the other; and $144$ sites placed uniformly at random instead of on a grid ($500$ null and $300$ alternative replicates with $\delta=0.2$ each). DA-P remains at or below the nominal level in every scenario ($0.8$--$4.6\%$), with coverage of $95$--$99\%$, as does Hering--Genton ($0.8$--$6.2\%$), whereas the paired $t$-test, the cluster procedures and DA-K over-reject in all of them ($7.6$--$19.6\%$). Misspecification costs power rather than validity: under the Gaussian covariance and heteroscedasticity, where the exponential model overstates the long-range correlation of the differentials, DA-P detects the gap in only $4\%$ of replicates, against $16\%$ for the (invalid) paired $t$-test.

\subsection{Range of dependence and the decision rule}\label{sec:sim1rule}

To examine how the procedures behave as the dependence range grows relative to the domain, and whether the range ratio can serve as a validity guard, we ran the design of this section with practical ranges $3a\in\{1.5,3,4.5,6,9,12,18\}$ grid units in space and the same values in time steps ($\phi=e^{-1/a}$), with $800$ null replicates per range ($300$ from a pilot run and $500$ from a confirmatory run on different seeds). Table~\ref{tab:range} shows that DA-P remains at or below the nominal level over the whole range ($1.8$--$3.4\%$), as does Hering--Genton ($2.1$--$5.5\%$), whereas the rejection rates of the paired $t$-test ($5.9\%$ to $25.6\%$), of the block bootstrap ($9.5\%$ to $38.9\%$) and of DA-K ($4.5\%$ to $14.4\%$) increase steadily with the range. Figure~\ref{fig:rule} pools all null replicates of the simulation studies and plots the rejection rate against the estimated range ratio: in Experiment~1 designs the rejection rate of DA-P does not increase with the range ratio, and in Experiment~2 its residual over-rejection is concentrated at intermediate ratios ($0.1$--$0.4$) rather than at large ones. A threshold on the range ratio, which we had intended to use to declare comparisons indeterminate, would therefore not improve the validity of DA-P; large range ratios signal low power (the size-adjusted power of every procedure is low at the strong level, Table~\ref{tab:exp1power}), not invalid inference.

The rule~\eqref{eq:rule}, which requires significance under both $\rho_0=1/K$ and $\rho_0=0.5$, is evaluated in Table~\ref{tab:rule} on the main replicates of both experiments. Its rate of false rejections is at most $4.0\%$ in Experiment~1 and in the absence of or under weak dependence in Experiment~2, and $8.7\%$ under moderate and strong dependence in Experiment~2, against $10.0\%$ and $11.0\%$ for DA-P alone. The price is that $0$--$11\%$ of the comparisons with a genuine gap are classified as indeterminate instead of significant. The rule therefore reduces, but does not eliminate, false claims of superiority in the most difficult setting; we discuss why in Section~\ref{sec:sim2}.

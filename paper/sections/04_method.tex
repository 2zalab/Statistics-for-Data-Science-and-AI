\section{Dependence-aware inference}\label{sec:method}

The proposed procedure, which we call DA-P (\emph{dependence-aware, parametric}), estimates the two components of~\eqref{eq:var} from the loss differentials of a single blocked cross-validation run. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} gives an overview: blocked cross-validation produces the loss differentials (stage~1); two parallel branches estimate the test-sampling variance, from a correlation model fitted to the variograms of the loss differentials, and the training-induced variance, from the dispersion of fold means (stage~2); the two are combined into a variance, a reference distribution, diagnostics and a three-way decision (stage~3). We also describe a kernel variant, DA-K, in which the test-sampling variance is a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) estimate; it was our initial proposal and is retained as a point of comparison. The steps are summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:dahac} and detailed below.

\subsection{Fold-centred differentials}

Let $m_k$ be the mean of the $d_i$ in fold $k$ and $\tilde u_i=d_i-m_{k(i)}$. Under~\eqref{eq:model}, $\tilde u_i=u_i-\bar u_{k(i)}$ is free of the fold effects, so that the dependence structure of $u$ can be studied without confusion with the training-induced component.

\subsection{Variogram model of the loss differentials}

The dependence range of the loss differential is in general different from that of the response: it is the range of the \emph{residual} structure that both models fail to capture. We therefore estimate it from $\tilde u$ directly. The directional empirical semivariograms
\begin{align*}
\hat\gamma_S(h)&=\frac{1}{2|N_S(h)|}\sum_{(i,j)\in N_S(h)}(\tilde u_i-\tilde u_j)^2,& N_S(h)&=\{(i,j):t_i=t_j,\ \|s_i-s_j\|\in I_h\},\\
\hat\gamma_T(\ell)&=\frac{1}{2|N_T(\ell)|}\sum_{(i,j)\in N_T(\ell)}(\tilde u_i-\tilde u_j)^2,& N_T(\ell)&=\{(i,j):s_i=s_j,\ |t_i-t_j|\in I_\ell\},
\end{align*}
are computed over distance and lag bins $I_h$, $I_\ell$ up to a maximum lag (by default half the spatial extent and a quarter of the temporal extent). For purely spatial data, $N_S(h)$ uses all pairs. To each we fit the exponential model with nugget $\gamma(h)=c_0+c_1\{1-\exp(-h/a)\}$ by the weighted least squares of Cressie~\cite{cressie1985}, with weights $|N(h)|/\gamma(h)^2$. This yields the correlation functions
\begin{equation}\label{eq:corr}
\hat g_S(h)=\frac{\hat c_{1,S}}{\hat c_{0,S}+\hat c_{1,S}}\,e^{-h/\hat a_S}\ (h>0),\qquad
\hat g_T(\ell)=\frac{\hat c_{1,T}}{\hat c_{0,T}+\hat c_{1,T}}\,e^{-\ell/\hat a_T}\ (\ell>0),
\end{equation}
with $\hat g_S(0)=\hat g_T(0)=1$, and the \emph{practical ranges} $\hat r=3\hat a$, the distances at which the correlation of the structured component falls to $5\%$ (set to zero when the structured part explains less than $5\%$ of the sill). The \emph{range ratio} $\max_q\hat r_q/L_q$, the largest practical range relative to the extent $L_q$ of the domain along the same axis, measures how many effectively independent regions the sample contains.

\subsection{Test-sampling variance}

\paragraph{DA-P (proposed).} The test-sampling variance and its within-fold analogues are computed from the fitted separable correlation $\hat g_{ij}=\hat g_S(\|s_i-s_j\|)\,\hat g_T(|t_i-t_j|)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vu}
\hat V_u=\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\hat g_{ij},\qquad
\hat V_k=\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{n_k^2}\sum_{i,j\in F_k}\hat g_{ij},\qquad \hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum_i\tilde u_i^2 .
\end{equation}
A product of exponential correlations with a nugget is positive definite, so $\hat V_u>0$. Pairs with $\hat g_{ij}<10^{-3}$ are neglected; the others are enumerated with a $k$-d tree, at a cost proportional to their number. Unlike a kernel estimator, \eqref{eq:vu} does not truncate the covariance at a bandwidth and does not multiply empirical cross-products, so it is neither biased downward by kernel down-weighting nor degenerate when the range approaches the fold size; its validity rests instead on the adequacy of the exponential model, which we examine under several misspecifications in Section~\ref{sec:sim1robust}. It is close in spirit to the spatial prediction comparison test of Hering and Genton~\cite{hering2011}, with two differences: the variogram is fitted to fold-centred differentials, and the training-induced component below is added.

\paragraph{DA-K (kernel variant).} With bandwidths $b_S=\hat r_S$ and $b_T=\hat r_T$, the product-Bartlett kernel
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel}
w_{ij}=\prod_{q=1}^{p}\Bigl(1-\frac{|s_{iq}-s_{jq}|}{b_S}\Bigr)_+\Bigl(1-\frac{|t_i-t_j|}{b_T}\Bigr)_+
\end{equation}
gives the HAC estimates $\hat V_u=n^{-2}\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}\tilde u_i\tilde u_j$ and $\hat V_k=n_k^{-2}\sum_{i,j\in F_k}w_{ij}\tilde u_i\tilde u_j$. Each factor is a one-dimensional Bartlett kernel, whose Fourier transform is non-negative, so the product is positive semi-definite and $\hat V_u\ge0$ (a Bartlett kernel applied to the Euclidean distance in two or more dimensions does not have this property).

\subsection{Training-induced variance}

Under~\eqref{eq:model}, the between-fold variance of fold means exceeds their within-fold test-sampling variance by the variance of the fold effects. We estimate it by the method of moments,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau}
\hat\tau^2=\max\Bigl\{0,\ s_m^2-\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K\hat V_k\Bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Since $\E[s_m^2]$ subtracts the covariance between fold effects (Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}), $\hat\tau^2$ targets $\tau^2(1-\rho)$ rather than $\tau^2$. The correlation $\rho$ is not identifiable from one cross-validation run~\cite{bengio2004}. For an assumed value $\rho_0$, the training component of~\eqref{eq:var} is $V_\eta=\tau^2[1/K+\rho_0(1-1/K)]=\zeta_K(\rho_0)\,\tau^2(1-\rho_0)$, with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zeta}
\zeta_K(\rho_0)=\frac{1/K+\rho_0(1-1/K)}{1-\rho_0},\qquad \zeta_K(1/K)=\frac1K+\frac1{K-1}.
\end{equation}
Following Nadeau and Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003}, our default is the test fraction $\rho_0=n_{\text{test}}/n=1/K$, for which $\zeta_K$ is exactly their inflation $1/J+n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$ with $J=K$ splits. The final variance estimate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vtotal}
\hat V=\hat V_u+\zeta_K(\rho_0)\,\hat\tau^2 .
\end{equation}

\subsection{What the variance estimator targets}

The following proposition makes explicit the conditions under which~\eqref{eq:vtotal} is unbiased, and the direction of its bias when they fail. For either estimator of the test-sampling variance, we consider the untruncated estimator $\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{raw}}=s_m^2-K^{-1}\sum_k\hat V_k$ and $\hat V_{\mathrm{raw}}=\hat V_u+\zeta_K(\rho_0)\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{raw}}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bias}
Under the working model~\eqref{eq:model} with equal fold sizes, let $\bar m^u_k$ be the fold means of $u$, $v_u=K^{-1}\sum_k\Var(\bar m^u_k)$, $c_u=[K(K-1)]^{-1}\sum_{k\neq l}\Cov(\bar m^u_k,\bar m^u_l)$, and let $\beta_u=\E[\hat V_u]-V_u$ and $\bar\beta=K^{-1}\sum_k\{\E[\hat V_k]-\Var(\bar m^u_k)\}$ be the biases of the kernel estimators. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bias}
\E[\hat V_{\mathrm{raw}}]-\Var(\hat\Delta)
=\underbrace{\beta_u-\zeta_K(\rho_0)\,(c_u+\bar\beta)}_{\text{test-sampling part}}
+\underbrace{\tau^2\Bigl[(1-\rho)\,\zeta_K(\rho_0)-\tfrac1K-\rho\bigl(1-\tfrac1K\bigr)\Bigr]}_{\text{training part}} .
\end{equation}
In particular, $\hat V_{\mathrm{raw}}$ is unbiased if (i) $\rho_0=\rho$, (ii) fold means of $u$ are uncorrelated across folds ($c_u=0$) and (iii) the kernel estimators are unbiased ($\beta_u=\bar\beta=0$). The training part is negative, i.e.\ the variance is underestimated, if and only if $\rho>\rho_0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The fold means are $m_k=\Delta_n+\eta_k+\bar m^u_k$ with $\eta$ independent of $u$, so their average variance is $\tau^2+v_u$ and their average covariance $\rho\tau^2+c_u$. By the identity in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}, $\E[s_m^2]=(1-\rho)\tau^2+v_u-c_u$, and by definition $\E[K^{-1}\sum_k\hat V_k]=v_u+\bar\beta$, hence $\E[\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{raw}}]=(1-\rho)\tau^2-c_u-\bar\beta$. With equal fold sizes, \eqref{eq:var} reads $\Var(\hat\Delta)=V_u+\tau^2[1/K+\rho(1-1/K)]$ and $\E[\hat V_u]=V_u+\beta_u$. Substituting gives~\eqref{eq:bias}. Writing $g(r)=1/K+r(1-1/K)$, the training part is $\tau^2[(1-\rho)g(\rho_0)/(1-\rho_0)-g(\rho)]$, which vanishes at $\rho=\rho_0$ and is strictly decreasing in $\rho$, with derivative $-\tau^2[g(\rho_0)/(1-\rho_0)+1-1/K]<0$.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} separates three sources of error, each of which is evaluated empirically in Section~\ref{sec:sim1}. \emph{Training correlation.} For $K=5$ and $\rho_0=1/K$, the training part equals $\tau^2(0.25-1.25\rho)$: assuming $\rho_0=0.2$ when $\rho=0.5$ leaves $0.375\tau^2$ of the $0.6\tau^2$ training variance unaccounted for, whereas $\rho<\rho_0$ errs on the conservative side. The sensitivity of the test to this choice is reported in Section~\ref{sec:sim1rho}. \emph{Cross-fold covariance.} $c_u$ is the covariance between test-sampling fluctuations of different folds; it is reduced by allocating whole blocks to folds and by buffers, and vanishes when blocks are larger than the dependence range. Because it enters with a negative sign, residual cross-fold dependence makes the test anti-conservative. \emph{Estimation bias of the test-sampling variance.} For DA-K, fold-centring removes the fold-level average of $u$ from $\tilde u$ and the Bartlett kernel down-weights covariances near the bandwidth; both make $\beta_u$ and $\bar\beta$ negative when the dependence range is not small relative to the blocks and to the domain. For DA-P, the biases come from the variogram fit (in particular from ranges that are too long to be estimated within the domain) and from misspecification of the exponential model. The term $-\zeta_K\bar\beta$ partially offsets $\beta_u$: the part of the fold-level variance missed by the within-fold estimators is recovered by $\hat\tau^2$. Finally, truncation at zero, $\hat\tau^2=\max\{0,\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{raw}}\}$, can only increase the variance, so $\E[\hat V]\ge\E[\hat V_{\mathrm{raw}}]$; its effect on the reference distribution is discussed next.

\subsection{Reference distribution}

HAC tests are known to over-reject when the effective sample size is small~\cite{kiefer2005}, and $\hat\tau^2$ rests on only $K-1$ degrees of freedom. We approximate the distribution of $\hat V$ by a scaled $\chi^2$ with Welch--Satterthwaite~\cite{satterthwaite1946} degrees of freedom
\begin{equation}\label{eq:df}
\hat\nu=\frac{\hat V^2}{\hat V_u^2/\nu_u+(\zeta_K\hat\tau^2)^2/(K-1)},\qquad
\nu_u=\min\Bigl\{\hat n_{\mathrm{eff}}-1,\ \prod_{q}\frac{3L_q}{2\hat r_q}\Bigr\},
\end{equation}
with $\hat n_{\mathrm{eff}}=n^{-1}\sum_i\tilde u_i^2/\hat V_u$ truncated to $[2,n]$, where the product runs over the spatial and temporal axes with a positive practical range, and $L_q$ is the extent of the domain along axis $q$. The second term of $\nu_u$ is the classical approximation to the degrees of freedom of a kernel spectral estimate~\cite{andrews1991} with a Bartlett window of width $\hat r_q$: for a lag window $k$ of bandwidth $b$ on a record of length $L$, $\Var(\hat V)/V^2\approx2(b/L)\int k^2$, with $\int k^2=2/3$, and the factors multiply across axes. It is used for both variants as an approximate count of the independent regions available to estimate the dependence, and caps the degrees of freedom when the range is not small compared with the domain. The test statistic $T=\hat\Delta/\sqrt{\hat V}$ is referred to a Student $t_{\hat\nu}$ distribution, and the $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval is $\hat\Delta\pm t_{\hat\nu,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat V}$.

When $\hat\tau^2_{\mathrm{raw}}\le0$ the training term vanishes and $\hat\nu=\nu_u$, which may be large even though only $K$ folds inform the training component. Truncation occurs when the observed dispersion of fold means is no larger than its predicted test-sampling part, that is, when the data show no evidence of a training effect; the variance itself is then not underestimated on average (Proposition~\ref{prop:bias}), but its sampling variability is understated. Section~\ref{sec:sim1} reports the rejection rate conditionally on truncation to check that this boundary case does not cause over-rejection.

\subsection{A three-way decision rule}\label{sec:rule}

Proposition~\ref{prop:bias} identifies the assumed training correlation $\rho_0$ as the one input of the procedure that cannot be checked from the data, and the one whose misspecification makes the test anti-conservative. We therefore attach to DA-P a decision rule with three outcomes that makes the dependence of the conclusion on $\rho_0$ explicit:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
\text{decision}=\begin{cases}
\text{reject } H_0 & \text{if } p(\rho_0=1/K)<\alpha \text{ and } p(\rho_0=0.5)<\alpha,\\
\text{indeterminate} & \text{if } p(\rho_0=1/K)<\alpha \le p(\rho_0=0.5),\\
\text{do not reject } H_0 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
An ``indeterminate'' outcome states that the apparent improvement is significant only if the training effects of different folds are assumed to be weakly correlated, and would not survive a correlation of $0.5$; it is not counted as evidence of superiority. Because $\zeta_K(\rho_0)$ is increasing in $\rho_0$, the second $p$-value is never smaller than the first.

We also report the range ratio $\max_q\hat r_q/L_q$ and the effective sample size $\hat n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as diagnostics of the \emph{information} available for the comparison. We initially intended to declare comparisons indeterminate when the range ratio exceeded a threshold, as suggested by the behaviour of DA-K; the simulations of Section~\ref{sec:sim1rule} show that, for DA-P, large range ratios reduce power but do not cause over-rejection, while the residual over-rejection of Experiment~2 occurs at intermediate ratios. A threshold on the range ratio would therefore not protect the validity of DA-P, and we do not use one.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{DA-P test for $H_0:\Delta_n=0$}\label{alg:dahac}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require loss differentials $d_i$, folds $k(i)$, coordinates $(s_i,t_i)$, level $\alpha$
\State $m_k\gets$ mean of $d_i$ over $F_k$; \ $\tilde u_i\gets d_i-m_{k(i)}$; \ $\hat\Delta\gets n^{-1}\sum_i d_i$
\State Fit exponential models to the spatial and temporal empirical variograms of $\tilde u$; correlations $\hat g_S,\hat g_T$ and ranges $\hat r_S,\hat r_T$
\State Compute $\hat V_u$ and $\hat V_1,\dots,\hat V_K$ by~\eqref{eq:vu} \Comment{DA-K: kernel~\eqref{eq:kernel} instead}
\State $\hat\tau^2\gets\max\{0,s_m^2-K^{-1}\sum_k\hat V_k\}$; \ $\hat V\gets\hat V_u+\zeta_K(\rho_0)\hat\tau^2$ \Comment{default $\rho_0=1/K$}
\State Compute $\hat n_{\mathrm{eff}}$, $\nu_u$ and $\hat\nu$ by~\eqref{eq:df}, and the range ratio $\max_q\hat r_q/L_q$
\State $p\gets2\,\Pr(t_{\hat\nu}>|\hat\Delta|/\sqrt{\hat V})$; interval $\hat\Delta\pm t_{\hat\nu,1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat V}$
\State Repeat lines 4--6 with $\rho_0=0.5$
\State \Return $p$, the interval, the diagnostics $(\hat r_S,\hat r_T,\hat n_{\mathrm{eff}},\max_q\hat r_q/L_q)$ and the decision~\eqref{eq:rule}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Properties}

\begin{remark}[Special cases]
If the fitted variograms show no structure ($\hat c_1=0$ in both directions) and $\hat\tau^2=0$, then $\hat V=n^{-2}\sum_i\tilde u_i^2$ and DA-P reduces to the one-sample $t$-test on the $d_i$; the same holds for DA-K with zero bandwidths. If the data have no spatial (or no temporal) coordinate, the corresponding factor of $\hat g_{ij}$ or of~\eqref{eq:kernel} is omitted. Both variance estimates are non-negative.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Consistency]
Under increasing-domain asymptotics, the DA-P variance is consistent when the exponential model is correctly specified and its parameters are consistently estimated~\cite{cressie1993}, and the DA-K variance is a Conley-type HAC estimator~\cite{conley1999}, consistent under mixing conditions when the bandwidths grow with, but more slowly than, the size of the domain~\cite{andrews1991}. Our variogram parameters are data-dependent and our variance includes a component, $\hat\tau^2$, estimated from only $K$ folds; we therefore do not claim exact asymptotic validity, and instead evaluate the finite-sample behaviour of the whole procedure by simulation with known truth.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
DA-P requires only the per-observation losses of the two models and their coordinates; it is agnostic to the learners, to the loss and to the blocking scheme. The same construction applies to any additive performance measure (squared error, absolute error, log loss, Brier score, 0--1 loss). Non-additive measures such as the AUC or the F1-score would require a linearisation (influence function) first.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Whether a machine learning model is ``better'' than another is a statistical claim, and on spatiotemporal data it depends on two choices that are rarely made explicit: the validation design, which defines what is estimated, and the inferential procedure, which defines how its uncertainty is assessed. We formalised the comparison of two learning procedures as inference on the mean of a dependent loss-differential field with correlated fold-level training effects, derived the bias of the paired $t$-test and of a combined variance estimator, and proposed DA-P, a test that adds to a variogram-based test-sampling variance a training-variance term and a Satterthwaite reference distribution, all computed from one blocked cross-validation run, together with a three-way decision rule that makes the dependence of the conclusion on the unverifiable training correlation explicit.

In controlled simulations, conventional procedures declared false improvements up to sixteen times more often than their nominal level, and random cross-validation could halve the estimated gain of a genuinely better model. DA-P kept the error rate at or below the nominal level in all designs with fixed error fields and weakly correlated training effects, including under anisotropy, a different covariance shape, heteroscedasticity, non-stationarity and irregular sampling, and it was the best procedure with refitted gradient-boosting learners, where the Hering--Genton test, designed for fixed predictions, failed. It was not exactly calibrated in that last setting under moderate and strong dependence, because a training component shared by all folds cannot be identified from a single cross-validation run, which also causes over-rejection when training effects are strongly correlated; we have characterised this limit rather than hidden it. On three public data sets, the conclusion of six of the nine comparisons depended on the validation design or on the test, and on hourly weather data the ranking of XGBoost and the random forest changed with the prediction task; the large improvements of tree ensembles over linear baselines on the hourly data were confirmed by every procedure.

Trustworthy machine learning requires not only accurate and explainable models but also trustworthy evidence that one model is better than another. We hope that the procedure, its diagnostics and the open-source implementation presented here will help practitioners in environmental, agricultural, epidemiological and mobility applications to report improvements that are statistically real, and to say so when the data cannot tell.

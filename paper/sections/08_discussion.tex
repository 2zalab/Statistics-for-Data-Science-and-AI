\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Are machine learning improvements statistically real?}

Our results give a nuanced answer to the question in the title. With spatially or temporally dependent data, the conventional pipeline (random $K$-fold cross-validation followed by a paired $t$-test, an i.i.d. bootstrap, or even the corrected resampled $t$-test) produces false discoveries at rates between $15\%$ and $40\%$ under moderate to strong dependence in our simulations, instead of the nominal $5\%$. Moreover, random cross-validation targets an interpolation estimand that can understate the gain at new places and times by half. Many reported ``significant'' improvements on such data are therefore not statistically supported, and some genuine improvements are misestimated.

Switching to blocked cross-validation fixes the estimand but not, by itself, the inference: applied to blocked folds, procedures that resample observations or that model only temporal dependence are \emph{more} anti-conservative than under random folds ($60$--$80\%$ false positives under strong dependence). Cluster procedures (block bootstrap, random-intercept models) are a clear improvement but remain anti-conservative whenever the dependence range is not small compared with the blocks, and they ignore the correlation of training-induced fold effects. The two procedures that best control the error rate are the simple paired $t$-test on blocked fold means, whose validity is explained by Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}, and DA-HAC.

\subsection{What DA-HAC adds, and what it does not}

DA-HAC was the procedure closest to the nominal level in Experiment~1 at three of the four dependence levels, and tied with the blocked paired $t$-test in Experiment~2. Its additional value over the paired $t$-test lies less in raw calibration than in four practical properties. (i)~It does not depend on the number of folds: the paired $t$-test with $K=5$ has four degrees of freedom whatever the sample size, and its validity hinges on the absence of correlation between fold means (Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}), which blocking only approximates. (ii)~It decomposes the uncertainty into a test-sampling component $\hat V_u$ and a training component $\zeta_K\hat\tau^2$, which tells the analyst whether more data or more training runs would sharpen the comparison. (iii)~It reports interpretable diagnostics: estimated dependence ranges, effective sample size and range ratio. (iv)~The ablations show that its two ingredients are both needed: without the training term its error rate is up to $2.4$ times larger, and a fixed bandwidth requires knowing a sensible dependence range in advance.

DA-HAC does not solve the problem completely. When the dependence range is a sizeable fraction of the domain (median range ratio above about $0.25$ in our experiments), DA-HAC rejected a true null in $10$--$15\%$ of cases, the blocked paired $t$-test in $11$--$18\%$, and the other procedures more often still. This is not a defect of a particular estimator but a limit of information: a sample that contains only a handful of effectively independent regions cannot tell whether a difference between two models is systematic or due to the particular realisation of the large-scale fields in that region. Fixed-$b$ asymptotics~\cite{kiefer2005} and our kernel-based degrees of freedom mitigate, but do not remove, this problem. In such cases the honest conclusion is that the data do not allow a reliable comparison, and the remedy is more data over a larger domain or longer period, not a different test.

\subsection{Recommendations}

From these results we derive the following recommendations for comparing two models on spatio-temporal data.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Define the estimand first.} Choose the validation design (random, spatial, temporal or spatio-temporal blocks, with buffers) to match the intended use of the model; random cross-validation answers an interpolation question.
\item \emph{Never rely on procedures that treat observations as independent} (i.i.d. bootstrap, $z$- or $t$-tests on pooled observations, McNemar's test) or on the paired $t$-test with random folds. Under dependence, the corrected resampled $t$-test is not a remedy either.
\item \emph{Use blocked cross-validation with a dependence-aware test.} Report DA-HAC together with the paired $t$-test on blocked folds; a conclusion that holds under both is more credible than one that holds under either. In our experiments, rejecting only when both tests reject reduced the error rate by $1$--$3$ percentage points at a small cost in power (an exploratory, post-hoc observation that would deserve a dedicated study).
\item \emph{Inspect the diagnostics.} Report the estimated ranges, the effective sample size and the range ratio. If the range ratio exceeds about $0.25$, or if the effective sample size is a few dozen, treat any $p$-value as optimistic and avoid strong claims.
\item \emph{Report intervals, not only $p$-values}, on the scale of the loss, as advocated for large data sets in this journal~\cite{caponero2025}: an improvement can be statistically real and practically negligible, or practically important and statistically unresolved.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and future work}

Our simulation designs are stylised: separable Gaussian fields on regular grids, stationary dependence, and a single pair of learners. The working model~\eqref{eq:model} assumes additive fold effects and stationarity of the loss differential, which may fail when one model is better in some regions and worse in others; a non-stationary extension with spatially varying $\Delta$ is a natural next step. The training correlation $\rho$ is set to the Nadeau--Bengio value rather than estimated, as it cannot be identified from a single cross-validation run~\cite{bengio2004}; repeated blocked cross-validation could provide information on it. We considered additive losses only; non-additive metrics such as the AUC or the F1-score would require linearisation. Finally, comparisons of more than two models raise multiplicity issues~\cite{demsar2006,benavoli2017} that combine with dependence and are left for future work.

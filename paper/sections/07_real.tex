\section{Case studies}\label{sec:real}

\subsection{Data and protocol}

We use three public data sets obtained from the Rdatasets collection~\cite{rdatasets}.

\paragraph{NYC weather.} Hourly weather records at the three New York City airports (EWR, JFK, LGA) in 2013, distributed with the \texttt{nycflights13} package~\cite{nycflights13} ($n=26{,}110$ after removing five records with missing temperature, humidity or wind speed). The task is to classify whether precipitation was recorded in the hour, from temperature, dew point, humidity, wind direction and speed, pressure, month, hour and airport; $6.7\%$ of hours are positive. The loss is the logarithmic loss.

\paragraph{Melbourne pedestrians.} Hourly pedestrian counts at four sensors in Melbourne in 2015--2016, distributed with the \texttt{tsibble} package~\cite{wang2020tsibble} ($n=66{,}037$). The task is to predict $\log(1+\text{count})$ from hour, day of week, month, day of year, year and sensor, under squared loss.

\paragraph{Las Rosas maize.} Yield-monitor data from a maize field in Las Rosas, Argentina, in 1999 and 2001~\cite{anselin2004}, distributed with the \texttt{agridat} package ($n=3443$, field of about $660\times380$\,m). The task is to predict yield from the nitrogen rate, brightness value, topographic zone, year and coordinates, under squared loss.

For the two hourly data sets, blocks are two-week windows ($26$ and $53$ blocks); as the airports lie within about $35$\,km of one another and the sensors within about $2$\,km, they are treated as co-located, so that all sites observed at the same time may be correlated, and a $24$-hour buffer is removed from the training set on each side of a test block. For the maize data, blocks are $75\times75$\,m tiles ($35$ non-empty blocks) with a $10$\,m buffer. We compare three learners pairwise: XGBoost ($300$ trees of depth $6$, learning rate $0.05$, row and column subsampling $0.8$), a random forest~\cite{breiman2001} ($200$ trees, minimum leaf size $5$, $50\%$ of features per split) and a linear baseline (logistic or ridge regression on standardised, mean-imputed features). All models use scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011} or the XGBoost library with default settings otherwise; no hyperparameter was tuned, since our object is the comparison, not the models.

\paragraph{Interpolation and forecasting.} Blocked cross-validation with blocks distributed over the whole period predicts each held-out window from data both before and after it; it answers an \emph{interpolation} question (filling gaps in a known record). For the two hourly data sets we therefore also use \emph{forward chaining}~\cite{tashman2000}: the $26$ or $53$ two-week blocks are grouped into $K=5$ consecutive segments, and each of the last four segments is predicted from all observations that precede it, minus a $24$-hour buffer, which answers a \emph{forecasting} question. The first segment is used for training only, the training sets are nested, and the same tests are applied to the $K-1=4$ test segments.

\paragraph{Multiplicity.} With three learners, each data set and validation design involves three pairwise comparisons. We report raw $p$-values and $p$-values adjusted by Holm's procedure~\cite{holm1979} within each data set, validation design and test, and we treat the case studies as illustrations of how conclusions depend on the inferential choices rather than as definitive rankings of the learners.

\paragraph{Seed-null diagnostic.} For each data set we also compare XGBoost with itself, the two runs differing only in their random seed ($30$ pairs). By symmetry, the expected loss differential of such a comparison is zero, while in each run the stochastic subsampling produces a non-zero observed difference. A procedure that frequently declares one seed ``significantly better'' than the other confuses training noise with a real improvement. Because the data are held fixed, this diagnostic measures the sensitivity of each procedure to training randomness only; it is not a full assessment of size, which Sections~\ref{sec:sim1} and~\ref{sec:sim2} provide.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:real} and Fig.~\ref{fig:forest} report the estimated loss differentials (first learner minus second; negative values favour the first), their $95\%$ intervals, and the $p$-values of the main procedures. Table~\ref{tab:realdiag} gives the DA-P diagnostics and the outcome of the three-way rule~\eqref{eq:rule}, and Fig.~\ref{fig:vario} the variograms of the fold-centred loss differentials.

\paragraph{NYC weather: the ranking depends on the question.} Under random cross-validation, XGBoost appears clearly better than the random forest (log-loss gain $0.011$) and than logistic regression ($0.041$), and the random forest better than logistic regression ($0.030$), with $p\le0.001$ for every procedure. Under two-week blocked cross-validation, i.e.\ for filling gaps of two weeks in the record, the three differences vanish ($|\hat\Delta|\le0.004$, DA-P $p\ge0.43$): random folds let the models recognise weather episodes from neighbouring hours of the same day, and the apparent superiority of XGBoost reflects its better use of this leakage. The i.i.d.\ bootstrap applied to the blocked differentials even declares the random forest significantly \emph{better} than XGBoost ($p=0.023$), a reversal produced by ignoring the $20$-hour dependence range of the loss differential. Under forward chaining, i.e.\ for forecasting later weeks from earlier ones, the random forest is better than XGBoost by $0.030$ (DA-P $p=0.008$, Holm-adjusted $0.023$, significant also with $\rho_0=0.5$), while the paired $t$-test, with three degrees of freedom, does not detect this difference ($p=0.050$, adjusted $0.15$). For XGBoost against logistic regression under forward chaining, the Hering--Genton test reports $p=0.036$ whereas DA-P, whose training term dominates the variance ($\hat\nu=4$), reports $p=0.42$: with nested training sets of very different sizes, the fold-to-fold variability of the comparison is large, and only a procedure that accounts for it avoids a spurious conclusion.

\paragraph{Melbourne pedestrians: a small gain of uncertain reality.} XGBoost and the random forest are indistinguishable under random cross-validation and under forward chaining, but under blocked cross-validation XGBoost has a small advantage ($0.0025$ in squared error of the log count). The paired $t$-test ($p=0.033$) and the i.i.d. bootstrap ($p=0.011$) call it significant; DA-P and Hering--Genton ($p=0.47$) and the cluster procedures ($p\ge0.40$) do not. The variogram of the loss differential has a short estimated range ($5$ hours), but pedestrian traffic also has daily and weekly cycles and holiday effects that an exponential model does not represent; we would conclude that the gain is not established. By contrast, the advantage of both tree ensembles over the linear baseline ($1.85$) is overwhelming under every procedure and validation design.

\paragraph{Las Rosas maize: limited information.} The gain of XGBoost over the random forest is $2.8$\,(q/ha)$^2$ under random cross-validation, significant with all procedures ($p\le0.013$), and much larger but much more uncertain under spatial blocking ($21$). The i.i.d. bootstrap ($p=0.001$) and the block bootstrap ($p=0.043$) declare it significant; the paired $t$-test ($p=0.31$), Hering--Genton ($p=0.51$) and DA-P ($p=0.46$) do not. The DA-P diagnostics explain why: the loss differential has a spatial range of about $150$\,m in a field of $660\times380$\,m (range ratio $0.40$), so that the $3443$ yield-monitor points carry the information of only about $20$ independent observations. With so little information, even the large gains over the linear model are only moderately significant: $p=0.011$ (adjusted $0.032$) for XGBoost, which survives the three-way rule, and $p=0.043$ (adjusted $0.086$) for the random forest, which does not survive the correction for multiplicity. Under random cross-validation all of these comparisons appear decisive.

\paragraph{Seed-null diagnostic.} Table~\ref{tab:seednull} reports how often each procedure declares one of two XGBoost runs, differing only by their seed, significantly better than the other. Procedures that resample observations independently do so in $33$--$87\%$ of the pairs under blocked cross-validation, and in $40\%$ of the pairs for the pedestrian data under random cross-validation, where the subsampling noise of the learner is temporally coherent. DM--HAC does so in $23$--$33\%$ and Hering--Genton in $20\%$ of the pedestrian pairs. DA-P ($0$--$3\%$) and the cluster procedures ($0$--$13\%$) are the least sensitive to training noise, followed by the blocked paired $t$-test ($0$--$13\%$). With $30$ pairs per data set, these rates have a Monte Carlo standard error of up to $9$ percentage points and should be read qualitatively.

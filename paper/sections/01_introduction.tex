\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Empirical machine learning advances one comparison at a time. A new gradient-boosting configuration is reported to beat a random forest, a deep architecture to beat gradient boosting, a feature set to beat another. Such claims rest on a small number of summary statistics (an average accuracy, an F1-score, a root mean squared error) computed by cross-validation, and, in the better cases, on a statistical test or a confidence interval for the difference. The test is meant to answer a simple question: \emph{is the observed improvement larger than what the randomness of the data and of the validation procedure could produce on its own?}

The standard tools for that question~\cite{dietterich1998,nadeau2003,demsar2006,benavoli2017} were developed for observations that are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). Much of the data on which machine learning is now used in practice is not. Meteorological, environmental, agricultural, epidemiological and mobility data are collected at locations and times that are close to one another, and nearby observations share unmeasured drivers. Two consequences are well documented. First, random $K$-fold cross-validation places near-duplicates of test observations in the training set, so that it measures interpolation rather than prediction at new places or times, and is optimistic when the latter is the target~\cite{roberts2017,ploton2020,meyer2018,bergmeir2012}. Second, the \emph{errors} of a fitted model are themselves dependent, so that $n$ test observations carry much less information than $n$ independent ones; this second problem has been addressed for fixed forecasts and spatial predictions~\cite{diebold1995,hering2011}, but rarely for learning procedures evaluated by cross-validation.

The first problem has a well-known remedy: blocked (spatial, temporal or spatio-temporal) cross-validation~\cite{roberts2017,valavi2019,linnenbrink2024}. The second problem is the subject of this paper. Blocking changes \emph{what} is estimated; it does not tell us \emph{how precisely} it is estimated. A practitioner who computes a blocked cross-validation score for two models and then applies a paired $t$-test, a bootstrap that resamples observations, or a test on fold-wise scores still relies on independence assumptions that the data violate. Whether an apparent improvement is ``statistically real'' then depends as much on these hidden assumptions as on the models.

Our starting point is the per-observation out-of-fold \emph{loss differential} $d_i = \ell(y_i,\hat f_A(x_i)) - \ell(y_i,\hat f_B(x_i))$ between two models $A$ and $B$. The mean of the $d_i$ estimates the expected gain of $A$ over $B$, and the comparison is a problem of inference on the mean of a dependent random field. Two sources of dependence must be handled at once. (i)~Test-point dependence: the $d_i$ inherit the spatial and temporal autocorrelation of the residuals of both models. (ii)~Training dependence: all observations of a fold are predicted by the same fitted models, and the models of different folds are fitted on overlapping training sets. Existing procedures handle one source at most. Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) variance estimators, as in the Diebold--Mariano test for forecasts~\cite{diebold1995}, and the parametric spatial prediction comparison test of Hering and Genton~\cite{hering2011} handle (i) for fixed predictors. The corrected resampled $t$-test of Nadeau and Bengio~\cite{nadeau2003} handles (ii) for i.i.d. data.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formalise model comparison under spatio-temporal dependence as inference on the mean of a loss-differential field with a fold-level random effect, and we show (Proposition~\ref{prop:pairedt}) that the widely used paired $t$-test on fold scores underestimates the variance by exactly the average covariance between fold means. This explains why it fails under random cross-validation even though it ``looks'' conservative with only $K-1$ degrees of freedom.
\item We propose \emph{DA-HAC}, a dependence-aware HAC procedure for comparing two models evaluated by blocked cross-validation. It combines (a) a product-Bartlett kernel estimator of the test-sampling variance whose spatial and temporal bandwidths are \emph{calibrated from the empirical variogram of the loss differentials themselves}; (b) a method-of-moments estimate of the training-induced fold variance, inflated by a Nadeau--Bengio factor; and (c) a Welch--Satterthwaite reference distribution whose degrees of freedom reflect the effective sample size. All three steps are computed from a single cross-validation run.
\item We assess the validity of conventional and dependence-aware procedures (paired $t$, i.i.d. bootstrap, corrected resampled $t$, block bootstrap, linear mixed model, Diebold--Mariano--HAC and DA-HAC) in two simulation studies with exact control of the truth: one in which loss-differential fields are generated directly, and one with gradient-boosting learners trained on spatio-temporal data in which two models have equal expected loss by an exchangeability argument.
\item We apply all procedures to three public data sets (hourly weather in New York, hourly pedestrian counts in Melbourne, and yield-monitor maize data in Argentina), including a ``seed-null'' diagnostic in which a model is compared with itself, and we derive practical recommendations. An open-source Python implementation is provided.
\end{enumerate}

The emphasis of the paper is thus not on whether spatial or temporal validation is preferable, a question already addressed in the literature, but on the \emph{validity of the inference} drawn from a given validation design. In the language of the call for this special issue, it is a contribution to measuring the trustworthiness of comparisons between machine learning models in real applications.
